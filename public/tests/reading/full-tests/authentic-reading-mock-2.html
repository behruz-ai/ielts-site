In Deep Water

Scientists are learning more about the sea floor around New Zealand

Select text to highlight, tap on it to clear

While we know that the sea is a big place, few of us realise just how big it really is. More than 66% of the world's surface is covered by ocean, and the sea makes up 97% of the environments on Earth that can support living things. The vast majority of this area - and New Zealand's seas are no exception - remains unexplored. In fact, the deepest parts of the oceans are so poorly known that nearly every submarine dive or research trawl (pulling up specimens from the seabed) brings new knowledge of genuine significance to oceanographers. Our present knowledge of land biodiversity (all the animals and plants) compared with ocean biodiversity is one measure of how early we are in the task of researching the deep sea. A good example is the New Zealand takahe bird. In 1948, a small population of takahe, a flightless bird which at the time was thought to be extinct, was found living in New Zealand's mountains. The rediscovery of this bird species created a sensation among bird lovers. In dramatic contrast, new species from New Zealand's deep sea are today being discovered at a rate of about one every two weeks.

What's more, catching, examining and naming a new species is only the first step in the learning process. Just because an animal has been given a scientific name and shelf space in a museum doesn't necessarily mean that we know anything at all about its lifestyle and ecology. Most deep-sea animals have never been seen alive, and the few that have were almost certainly acting unnaturally in the glare of lights from the submarine camera with which they were observed. For this reason, our understanding of life in the deep sea relies heavily on conjecture: fierce debate continues among scientists over such details as how fast giant squid can swim, and why lantern fish flash their little lights. In some respects, deep-sea biology parallels the way that detectives hunt for criminals, requiring comparable problem-solving and clue-finding skills.

The sea floor around New Zealand has a wide range of seabed features. One of the most important are seamounts, which are underwater volcanoes. Most of the world's charted seamounts were discovered in the past 40 years, although the exact number around New Zealand is not known. One of the most conspicuous is the Bollons Seamount, which among others was visited by a scientific expedition in 2002. It rises more than three kilometres above the surrounding seabed, and measures two hundred kilometres across, making it a remarkable size, and therefore of particular interest.

Research has shown that these mountains of the deep are home to many species, although it is not clear why. One theory is that the ocean currents cause plankton, which is an important source of food, to thrive on their upper surfaces. Another factor is that they are not completely draped with the fine mud that covers just about everything else in the deep sea. Instead, their rocky surfaces provide attachment points for animals like sponges and anemones. Many seamounts are found on the Chatham Rise, which is a huge platform in a remote part of the ocean. The top of the rise is covered with small seamounts, many of which have been named by the inhabitants of the nearby Chatham Islands. They come fishing here because a valuable species called orange roughy seasonally migrates to these shallow waters to spawn.

Canyons are another of New Zealand's underwater features. These trenches cut deeply into the sea floor and come unusually close to land. At Goose Bay, the squid that live in the canyon attract sperm whales, and every year thousands of people come to see the whales just off the coast, helping local tourism. Further south, near the city of Dunedin, several canyons are found. Since the 1960s, the University of Otago's ship Nerida has conducted research in this area, leading to many unusual discoveries. Among these was a population of button-sized coral, whose remains had previously been mistaken by early fossil collectors for small dinosaur bones.

Scientists believe that canyons were created when powerful underwater currents cut into the seabed. Nearly all submarine canyons are located offshore from a large river, suggesting that it was the force of this river from the land into the sea that set the underwater current in motion. However, there are other explanations for canyons in other parts of the world, and a spectacular example took place in November 1929, when a significant earthquake in Canadian waters triggered an underwater current that travelled more than 720 kilometres, creating a canyon and cutting an undersea communication cable in the process.

Research in the seas around New Zealand has resulted in a far better understanding of the seamounts, canyons and other underwater features that stand out from the otherwise flat sea floor. Indeed, the seabed generally is very flat compared to the land. To deal with this, oceanographers use a technique called 'vertical exaggeration' in their maps, to make changes in ocean depth more apparent. Unfortunately, this has resulted in many people believing that the seabed is much steeper than it really is. But this misunderstanding aside, we know much more about the deep ocean today than we did even 20 years ago, and the speed of scientists' discoveries is only likely to increase.

Lean Production – innovation in manufacturing systems

How Toyota's post-war manufacturing philosophy reshaped industry worldwide

Select text to highlight, tap on it to clear

14

A After the First World War, car makers Henry Ford and Arthur Sloan of General Motors moved world manufacturing from centuries of craft production into the age of mass production. Largely as a result of this, the United States soon dominated the world economy. After the Second World War, and approximately a hundred years after Japan opened up to the modern world, Eiji Toyoda and Taiichi Ohno pioneered the concept of lean production at the Toyota car company. And now, although superimposing the method on existing mass production systems causes pain and upheaval, manufacturers around the world are trying to embrace this innovative system.

15

B Perhaps the best way to describe lean production is to compare it with the two other major manufacturing systems: craft production and mass production. The craft producer uses highly skilled workers and simple but flexible tools to make exactly what the customer asks for one item at a time. A present-day example of this method is the customised production of a few exotic sports cars. The concept of craft production remains very popular, but the problem with it is obvious. Goods produced by the craft method—as cars once exclusively were—cost too much for most of us to afford. So at the beginning of the twentieth century, mass production was developed as an alternative method. The mass producer uses narrowly skilled professionals to design products which are then made by unskilled or semi-skilled workers, using expensive, single-purpose machines. These churn out standardised products in very high volumes. Because the machinery costs so much and is so intolerant of disruption, the mass producer keeps standard designs in production for as long as possible. The result is that the customer gets lower costs but at the expense of variety, and by means of work methods which most employees find boring and dispiriting. By contrast, the lean production system combines the advantages of craft and mass production, while avoiding the high cost of the former and the rigidity of the latter. Towards this end, companies appoint teams of multi-skilled workers to all levels of the organisation, and use highly flexible and increasingly automated machines to produce goods in enormous volume and variety.

16

C Lean production is so called because, compared with mass production, it uses less of everything—half the human effort in the factory, half the manufacturing space, half the investment in tools, and half the engineering hours to develop the new product. It also results in far fewer defects.

17

D Perhaps the most striking contrast between mass and lean production systems lies in their production standards. Mass producers set a limited goal for themselves: 'good enough', which translates into an acceptable number of defects, a maximum acceptable number of inventories, and a narrow range of standardised products. Lean producers, on the other hand, are unwilling to compromise standards in any of these areas.

18

E Although cost reduction is the primary objective of the lean production system, it must meet three other intermediate objectives in order to achieve this: quantity control, quality assurance and respect for humanity. Firstly, the system must be able to adapt to daily and monthly fluctuations in demand. Secondly, each separate process must supply only good units to the subsequent process. Thirdly, in as far as the system uses human resources to attain its cost objectives, respect for human needs must be cultivated. It should be emphasised that none of these three objectives can be achieved separately.

19

F The continuous flow of lean manufacturing production relies on two practical mechanisms: just-in-time and autonamation. Just-in-time means, for example, that in the process of assembling the parts to build a car, components from the preceding process should arrive at the next part of the line at exactly the right time and in the correct quantities. If just-in-time is fully realised throughout the company, then superfluous inventories are completely eliminated from the factory, making stores or warehouses unnecessary. However, relying solely on a central planning approach to control schedules for all stages of the production process simultaneously is very difficult in the case of cars, which consist of thousands of parts. So the lean system looks at the production flow in reverse; in other words, employees go to the preceding process to withdraw the necessary quantity of units at the appropriate time. The preceding process must produce only sufficient quantities of units to replace those that have been withdrawn, and in turn withdraw the requisite number of components from the process that precedes it.

20

G Autonamation is the automatic checking for abnormalities in the production process. In order to realise just-in-time perfectly, only units which are in perfect condition must be allowed to flow to the next process, and this flow must be regular and uninterrupted. In other words, quality control must coexist with just-in-time procedures throughout the system. Autonamation involves building in a mechanism to prevent the multiplication of defects in machines or product lines. For example, in Toyota factories almost all the machines have been fitted with stopping devices, and the concept of autonamation has been extended to manual production lines. If something abnormal happens there, the worker pushes a button to stop the whole line, and lights, which are hung so high in the factory that they are visible to everyone, indicate the position of the problem.

The contradictions of tourism

Weighing the economic promise of tourism against its cultural and environmental costs

Select text to highlight, tap on it to clear

Tourism may bring benefits to a society but its effects are not always straightforward. International tourism presents itself as straightforward, passive and benign, yet it is complex, interesting and important, full of contradictions and depths. It has enormous growth potential; almost every country desires an expansion in this sector. Yet whether and how to grow is not nearly as straightforward as one might hope.

Tourism can provide the ideal export for developing countries, which can capitalize on their unique cultures and natural environments. However, to do this they need to have features such as airports, hotels, sanitation facilities, roads and medical facilities. A government can build these itself, using revenue from local taxes or getting finance from foreign lenders. Alternatively, the government may attract foreign investment, allowing others to pay for the building, thus relieving itself of providing capital but forgoing profits. Tourism is, however, a risky investment. It can gradually decline as a resort becomes seen as outdated, or it can be destroyed overnight by a single hurricane, or a political emergency. Even when successful, countries and localities are faced with a dilemma: keep it within limits, or extend its benefits but also deepen dependence on a single, fickle industry.

By definition, tourists travel to see and knowingly to encounter the different, the original, the authentic. In this increasingly global world, what they want is regional and unique. But only through artifice can the tourist demand for authenticity be met. An event such as a festival or ceremony needs to be brought to their attention and its importance and unique nature elaborated to them. Natural sites, too, need to be presented if they are to be regarded as noteworthy; gates, permits, and notices are required to set them apart from the mundane. Complicated framing and staging allows tourists to recognize, by sight, 'the actual thing'.

Tourism places a premium on the parts of a culture that are visible, such as handicrafts and costume, and disregards those that are not, like the rules governing seating on a bus, attitudes to the elderly, or appropriate ways of addressing family members; in this way, tourism favors what can be commodified. Yet commodities can be produced most efficiently using industrial machinery to take advantage of economies of scale, an option closed to village artisans. This alienates the producers from what is being produced, and means the product no longer has any personal meaning to them.

In addition, cultural idiosyncrasies and natural spectacles change over time. Tourists can't be attracted, however, to an unstable and ambiguous culture, or to a natural site that is in the process of transforming itself. As a consequence, efforts are made to fix and standardize: folk dances, tortilla-making, peace pipes, bird habitats and the course of the Amazon have all been bound and defined. Had they not been fixed, however, they would not be accessible.

Tourism also promotes an external orientation. Like other exports, the 'tourism experience' is produced by local people, and consumed by foreigners. To obtain loans and ultimately create revenue, a government (of whatever size) has to please outside investors and potential travelers. In these ways, tourism can result in a political system becoming more authoritarian and giving less consideration to the needs of individuals within the country.

In recent years, with increased awareness of some of these dangers, the phenomenon of ecotourism has developed. This has been seen as a way to prevent and reverse the environmental degradation resulting from tourism as well as from general human activity. Ecotourists distance themselves from mainstream hotel tourists, and also from adventure tourists like those who have, for example, left rubbish at the base of Mount Everest in Nepal. They want to do things like sleep in tents on platforms in tropical rainforests, swim among the fish on coral reefs, or walk over a tree canopy on a net bridge. They value the ecosystem as it is and are willing to pay for its preservation. In this way, they create an incentive for local people to refrain from using natural resources for short-term gain.

However, what is fortunate for the birds is not always so fortunate for the local people. In order to be environmentally sustainable, ecotourists have to be few in number. The economies of scale that allow tourism to be profitable cannot operate in a small bush camp. Camping doesn't generate much revenue. Ecotourism proves that tourism does not have to be in conflict with environmental preservation. Unfortunately, it also proves that the realm in which the environment and the economy can be mutually supporting is tiny.

Furthermore, Westerners' interest in biodiversity often means that the tourist wants to listen to a parrot, not a local elder, and that dollars bypass beggars on their way to support trees. Thus ecotourists' environmental awareness can stand in contrast to their indifference to the local human community. Tourism always contains a message: this is what we value, this is what we don't.
